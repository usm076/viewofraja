If you buy your own health insurance, the clock is already running. This year's ACA open enrollment window is shorter than it used to be, about six weeks starting November 1, which puts the deadline in mid December on [HealthCare.gov](http://HealthCare.gov). Some state exchanges run on their own schedules, so check the exact date for yours.

Reports ahead of this enrollment season also pointed to higher premiums, some insurers leaving certain markets, and changes to financial help. Put together, that means this is a year where simply letting your plan renew on autopilot could cost you real money.

This guide walks through how to compare plans and which mistakes cost people the most. It covers marketplace plans, employer plans and Medicare briefly, since each has a different calendar.

## First, Which Open Enrollment Applies to You?

There is no single deadline. It depends on how you get your coverage:

- **ACA marketplace ([HealthCare.gov](http://HealthCare.gov) or your state exchange):** about six weeks from November 1 this year. Confirm your exchange's last day before you plan around it.
- **Employer plans:** your company sets its own window, usually in the fall. Check your HR portal or benefits email now, since some close within days.
- **Medicare:** the annual enrollment period runs October 15 to December 7. If you're on Medicare, this is when you can switch Medicare Advantage or Part D drug plans.
- **Medicaid and CHIP:** these have no open enrollment period. You can apply at any time if you qualify.

If you miss your window, you generally can't change plans until next year unless you have a qualifying life event, such as losing other coverage, moving, getting married or having a baby.

## Why You Shouldn't Just Let Your Plan Renew

Many people keep their current plan year after year. This year that's riskier than usual, for three reasons:

**Prices may have changed a lot.** Insurers adjust premiums every year, and the changes for 2027 were widely reported as increases. Your plan could cost noticeably more than last year even though nothing about your coverage changed.

**Your plan may no longer be available.** When insurers leave a market or discontinue a plan, you can be moved into a different one automatically, with a different network and different drug coverage.

**Your financial help may be different.** The rules for premium subsidies have shifted in recent years, so the amount you got last year isn't a safe guide. Use the exchange's calculator to see what you qualify for in 2027.

## How to Compare Plans: The Four Numbers That Matter

A lower monthly premium doesn't mean a cheaper plan. When you compare options, look at all four of these:

**1. Premium.** What you pay every month, whether you use care or not.

**2. Deductible.** What you pay yourself before the plan starts sharing costs for most services. A plan with a $0 premium and a $7,000 deductible can leave you paying thousands before it helps.

**3. Copays and coinsurance.** What you pay per visit or per bill after the deductible. Check the cost of the care you actually use: primary care, specialists, prescriptions and emergency visits.

**4. Out of pocket maximum.** The most you'd pay in a year for covered care, besides your premiums. This is your worst case number. If a serious illness hit, this is the figure you'd need to be able to afford.

A useful way to compare is to estimate your realistic total cost for the year: twelve months of premiums plus the care you expect to use, then compare that with your worst case cost, which is premiums plus the out of pocket maximum.

## Bronze, Silver, Gold or Platinum?

Marketplace plans come in metal tiers. The metal tells you how costs are split between you and the insurer, not how good the care is.

- **Bronze:** lowest premiums, highest deductibles. This can make sense if you're healthy and mainly want protection against a big, unexpected bill.
- **Silver:** a middle ground. It also has one major advantage, covered below.
- **Gold and platinum:** higher premiums, lower costs when you use care. These can pay off if you have regular medical needs, ongoing prescriptions or a planned procedure.

**The silver plan advantage.** If your income is low enough to qualify for cost sharing reductions, those extra savings, which lower your deductible and copays, are only available on silver plans. For some households a silver plan ends up with better coverage than a gold plan at a lower cost. The exchange will show you if you qualify.

## Check These Before You Choose

Several details can make a "cheap" plan expensive:

- **Is your doctor in network?** Search for your specific doctors and hospitals on the plan's directory, then confirm with the doctor's office. Out of network care can cost far more or may not be covered at all.
- **Are your prescriptions covered?** Look up each medication in the plan's drug list, called a formulary, and note which tier it's on, since tier decides what you pay.
- **Do you need referrals?** Some plan types require a referral from a primary care doctor before you see a specialist.
- **What does the plan do in an emergency or out of state?** This matters if you travel or live near a state border.

## Can an HSA Help You Save?

If you choose a high deductible health plan that qualifies, you may be able to open a Health Savings Account (HSA). Contributions go in before tax, grow tax free and can be withdrawn tax free for qualified medical costs. Unlike many similar accounts, the money rolls over every year and stays yours.

An HSA can be a good fit if you're generally healthy and can cover the deductible from savings. If you have a lot of regular medical costs and little cash set aside, a high deductible can become a burden. ## Common Mistakes That Cost People Money

- **Picking by premium alone.** The cheapest monthly price often comes with the highest deductible.
- **Ignoring the out of pocket maximum.** It's your real worst case cost.
- **Forgetting to update your income estimate.** If your income changed this year, update it on your application so your subsidy matches. An outdated number can mean owing money back at tax time.
- **Skipping the drug list.** A plan that doesn't cover your medication on a good tier can erase any savings.
- **Waiting until the last day.** Websites get busy near deadlines, and you may need time to compare and ask questions.

## Ways to Lower Your Health Insurance Costs

- Compare every plan on the exchange, not just the one you have.
- Check whether you qualify for financial help or cost sharing reductions.
- Consider whether a lower premium plan fits your actual health needs.
- Use an HSA if you're eligible and can afford the deductible.
- Look at free help in your area. Navigators and certified enrollment counselors can walk you through plans at no charge.

## The Bottom Line

With a shorter window and shifting prices, this is a year to compare, not to coast. Set aside an hour, work out your realistic yearly cost under two or three plans, check that your doctors and prescriptions are covered, and enroll well before the deadline.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**When is open enrollment for 2027 health insurance?** For the ACA marketplace, the window is about six weeks starting November 1. Check your exchange for its exact last day. Employer deadlines vary, and Medicare's annual enrollment runs October 15 to December 7.

**What happens if I miss open enrollment?** You usually have to wait until next year, unless you qualify for a special enrollment period because of a life event such as losing other coverage, moving, marriage or a birth.

**Is a bronze plan or a silver plan better?** It depends on your health and income. Bronze has lower premiums and higher deductibles. Silver can be better if you qualify for cost sharing reductions, which are only available on silver plans.

**What is the difference between a deductible and an out of pocket maximum?** The deductible is what you pay before the plan starts sharing costs. The out of pocket maximum is the most you'll pay in a year for covered care, which acts as your worst case limit.

**Are health insurance premiums going up in 2027?** Reports ahead of this enrollment season pointed to higher premiums in many markets, though the amount varies by state and insurer. Compare plans on your exchange to see your actual prices.
