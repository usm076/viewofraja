Bitcoin can move 10% in a day. That's exciting for traders, but not much use if you just want to hold or send money. Stable coins exist to solve that. They're crypto tokens designed to stay worth one dollar, so you get the speed of crypto without the price swings.

That promise has made stable coins one of the most widely used parts of the crypto world. It has also made them the subject of serious regulatory attention, because a coin that's supposed to be steady can fail in ways that hurt people. This guide explains how stable coins work, what backs them, and how safe they really are.

## What Is a Stable coin?

A stable coin is a cryptocurrency designed to keep a steady value, most often $1 for each token. You can hold it in a crypto wallet, send it to anyone in the world within minutes, and trade it for other crypto.

Most stable coins aim to match the US dollar, though some track the euro, gold or other assets. The key idea is the "peg": the issuer tries to make sure one token can always be exchanged for roughly one dollar.

## How Stable coins Keep Their Value

There are three main approaches, and the difference matters because each has different risks.

**1. Fiat backed stable coins.** The issuer holds real assets, such as cash and short term government bonds, to back each token. In theory, if you hold 100 tokens, there are about $100 in reserves behind them. The best known stable coins, including Tether (USDT) and USD Coin (USDC), work this way.

**2. Crypto backed stable coins.** These are backed by other cryptocurrencies, held in smart contracts. Because crypto is volatile, they usually hold more value in reserves than the stable coins they issue, as a cushion.

**3. Algorithmic stable coins.** These try to hold the peg through code and market incentives rather than holding reserves. This is the riskiest design. The Terra USD collapse in 2022 wiped out tens of billions of dollars in value and showed how fast a pegged coin can fail when confidence disappears.

## What People Use Stable coins For

- **Trading.** Traders move into stable coins to step out of price swings without leaving the crypto system.
- **Sending money abroad.** Transfers can be faster and cheaper than traditional international wires, especially in countries with limited banking access.
- **Payments and business use.** More companies are testing stable coins for settlement and payments.
- **Holding dollars in countries with unstable currencies.** In some places, stable coins serve as a way to hold a more stable currency.
- **Earning yield.** Some platforms pay interest on stable coin deposits, though this carries extra risk, covered below.

## Are Stable coins Safe?

It depends on the type, the issuer, and what you do with them. A stable coin is safer than a volatile crypto, but it isn't the same as money in a bank. The main risks are:

**Reserve risk.** The peg is only as strong as the assets behind it. If an issuer doesn't actually hold enough, or holds assets that are hard to sell quickly, it may not be able to pay everyone back at $1.

**Dep eg risk.** Even well backed stable coins can briefly lose their peg. In March 2023, USDC fell well below $1 for a short time after a bank where its issuer held some reserves failed. It recovered, but holders who panicked and sold took losses.

**No deposit insurance.** Money in a bank account is typically insured by the FDIC up to certain limits. Stable coins are not. If the issuer fails, there's no government guarantee.

**Issuer and transparency risk.** You're trusting the company to hold and report its reserves honestly. Look for regular third party attestations or audits, and be cautious about issuers that don't publish them clearly.

**Platform risk.** If you keep stable coins on an exchange or lending platform, you also depend on that platform staying solvent. High advertised yields on stable coin deposits often mean someone is taking risk with your money.

**Regulatory risk.** Rules are still developing, and changes can affect how stable coins operate.

## What the Law Says

The US passed a federal law for payment stable coins in 2025, known as the GENIUS Act. In general terms, it sets requirements for issuers around holding reserves, disclosures and oversight. Parts of it are still being phased in, so details may change as agencies write the rules.

## USDT vs USDC: A Quick Comparison

The two largest dollar stable coins are often compared:

- **Tether (USDT)** is the largest and most widely traded. It has faced more questions over time about how transparent its reserves are.
- **USD Coin (USDC)** is issued by a US regulated company and is generally seen as more transparent about its reserves, though it too dep egged briefly in 2023.

Neither is risk free. If you hold either, check the issuer's latest reserve reports rather than relying on reputation alone.

## How to Use Stable coins More Safely

- **Stick to established, transparent issuers.** Look for regular published reserve reports.
- **Avoid algorithmic stable coins.** The design has failed badly before.
- **Don't chase high yields.** If a platform pays far more than a savings account on stable coins, ask where the return comes from.
- **Don't keep more than you need on one platform.** Spread risk across wallets and providers.
- **Use a wallet you control for larger amounts.** An exchange failure can freeze your funds.
- **Watch out for scams.** Scammers often use stable coin names or fake "pegged" coins. Our guide on [spotting fake meme coins](https://financewithraja.com/posts/avoid-fake-meme-crypto-coins/) covers the red flags.

## Do Stable coins Have Tax Consequences?

In many countries, including the US, trading one crypto for another, including swapping into or out of a stable coin, can be a taxable event, even though the value barely changes. Keep records of your transactions. Our [crypto tax guide](https://financewithraja.com/posts/crypto-tax-guide-2025/) explains what to track.

## Are Stable coins Right for You?

They can be useful if you move money internationally, trade crypto or need a dollar linked asset, but they're not a replacement for a bank account or savings. For everyday savings, an insured account is usually safer. If you're new to crypto, start with our [beginner's guide](https://financewithraja.com/posts/cryptocurrency-guide-beginners-2025/) before putting money into anything.

## The Bottom Line

Stable coins are a useful tool and one of the most practical parts of crypto, but "stable" is a goal, not a guarantee. The safest ones are backed by transparent, liquid reserves and run by regulated, well known issuers. The riskiest rely on code alone or promise high returns.

Treat them as a tool, understand who is holding the reserves, and don't treat a token worth $1 as money in an insured bank.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is a stable coin?** A cryptocurrency designed to hold a steady value, usually one US dollar per token, so you can use crypto without big price swings.

**Are stable coins safe?** They're safer than volatile cryptocurrencies, but they carry risks: reserve problems, dep egging, no deposit insurance and platform failure. Safety depends on the issuer and how you hold them.

**Can a stable coin lose its value?** Yes. Some have briefly dropped below $1, and algorithmic ones have collapsed completely.

**Are stable coins insured?** No. Unlike bank deposits, stable coins aren't covered by FDIC insurance.

**What is the difference between USDT and USDC?** They are the two largest dollar stable coins. USDC is issued by a US regulated company and is seen as more transparent about reserves, while USDT is larger and has faced more questions about its reserves. Both carry risk.

**Do I pay taxes on stable coins?** In many countries, trading into or out of a stable coin can be a taxable event. Keep records and check your local rules.
